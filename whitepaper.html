Aligned equity: public goods at the point of formation

Part I

Introduction

The state of public goods

We are in a crisis of how public goods get created. The crisis is not only economic but conceptual. We’ve lost a working account of what public goods are, who pays for them, and what they owe us.

Centuries of economic theory define public goods by two properties inherent to the good itself. Non-rivalry: one person's consumption does not diminish what is available to anyone else. Non-excludability: the supplier cannot prevent non-payers from consuming. The definition is precise and useful, but it describes the conditions under which a market fails to reliably supply a good. It does not describe what the good does, why it matters, or who should provide it.

To say we are in a crisis of how public goods get created is an argument about institutional design and incentives rather than a moral or strictly economic one.

What we mean by public goods

This paper does not interrogate the economic argument of public goods exclusively. When we use the phrase, we mean goods, services, and utilities that support overall societal health and wellbeing — be it clean air provisions, a cure for Alzheimer's, wealth-generating opportunities for employees, or the correction of externalities an enterprise has itself produced.

We recognize that the public should ideally hold authorship over that definition. Our scope is deliberately limited as to not opine on authorship or decisions of allocation.

What we do intend to explore is the mechanism itself. The instrument we present later in this paper generates public goods in three ways: by creating something net new, by changing where upside flows — sharing profit with stakeholders or subsidizing access — or by working against externalities the enterprise generates. The instrument is specific in design and flexible in outcome.

The settlement that assumed automatic aggregation

Through the middle of the twentieth century, the state was the primary funder, and at times direct provider, of public goods through taxation and expanded welfare systems.1 Charitable capital was expected to play a residual role: funding experimental or minority causes that the government was structurally unable to support, and gap-filling where the state and market did not reach.2 Corporations created private economic value, and their contribution to the public was assumed to flow indirectly — through wages, taxes paid, and the goods and services they sold.

Beneath the whole arrangement sat one load-bearing assumption: automatic aggregation. Private value creation, plus redistributive taxation, plus charitable topping-up, would net out to a fair society.

That settlement broke because the intermediation faltered.3 As public capacity has thinned and confidence in it has waned,4 and as wealth has concentrated in private hands at a scale without precedent,5 private power has been left holding the position those public institutions used to occupy — controlling the resources whose deployment now determines whether public goods get made — without having chosen that role or built the means to exercise it.6

Philanthropy, meanwhile, has been asked to carry weight it was never built to bear. Philanthropy’s defensible role is to promote pluralism and discovery — decentralizing who gets to define a good and funding long-horizon experiments that neither electorates nor markets can afford. Philanthropy was never designed to supply public goods at scale, and expecting it to do so has contributed to this breakdown.

It has proven insufficient not by the measure of its own activity, which is at record levels, but by the measure that matters: capital has accumulated inside the philanthropic system faster than the problems it was meant to address have improved.7 Because the tax benefit is realized at the moment of contribution, the public benefit is optional and can be indefinitely deferred — by design.8 And the professional apparatus guiding great wealth is compensated on assets under advisement rather than on gifts granted, so the default counsel is to preserve and grow capital rather than to move it.

The deeper issue is structural, not one of volume. The institutions that philanthropy funds — foundations, nonprofits — are built for service delivery and for acting on value after it has been created, extracted, and privatized. They operate at the altitude of the symptom, with no leverage over the capital structures, governance, or incentives of the enterprises that actually generate the value, and the harm, upstream.

Public goods in the 21st century

The institution now best equipped to produce public goods is neither the agency nor the foundation. It is the corporation.

The corporation’s capacity to organize talent, compound expertise, distribute at scale, and convert resources into outcomes efficiently has no rival among the institutions we have traditionally charged with the public good; in this moment, the corporate form is simply the most effective public-goods-producing technology available to us.

The shift has already happened. The mechanisms built today determining the wellbeing and protection of the public — AI, biotech, national security, energy generation and storage, grid infrastructure, healthcare distribution — are already being built by private companies with private capital. In each, the choice of what gets built, and for whom, is made inside a private firm.

That this is so is not a triumph but a symptom — a measure of how far the public institutions we built for the purpose have been allowed to erode. And it is a claim about capability, not authority: the corporation is the best engine we have, not the best steer. Left to its own logic it will aim that capacity wherever profit points.

The result

The result is a double bind. The institutions with the capacity to create public value at scale — operating private enterprises, with their expertise, distribution, talent, and capital efficiency — are governed by a default mandate that treats public benefit as a leakage of the returns they seek to produce. The institutions with the mandate to create public goods — foundations and nonprofits — lack the capital, scale, and market discipline of enterprises.

Capacity without mandate on one side; mandate without capacity on the other.

Large pools of capital sit disconnected from the institutions best positioned to deliver public goods, at the precise moment those goods are most needed — not by accident of circumstance, but by the design of the system that routes value between them.

Public goods at the point of formation

What enterprises lack, then, is not the capability to produce public goods but the license to — a mandate that permits it and capital that asks it to turn that capacity toward public ends. The direction has to come from capital holders deliberately choosing the kind of return they want. But discretion exercised once — by one capital holder or one founder — is not a scalable structure, as it has to be continuously rebuilt alongside each bespoke scenario.9

The missing infrastructure is not a better philanthropy or a more disciplined impact fund. It is the connective tissue between public-minded capital and the corporate form itself — the mechanisms and intermediaries that let asset holders direct capital into enterprises as enterprises, harnessing the very engine that makes them formidable, to produce public goods on purpose rather than by accident.

Public benefit is an afterthought in every existing vehicle because it is decided downstream of value: created, accumulated, and only then, optionally, transferred to the public. With the design of a new instrument, the correction can sit at formation — before the value exists, an obligation can be made by agreement rather than intention.

Private allocation of public goods is already occurring — it is just unstructured. An agreement built at formation is more accountable and efficient than discretion exercised later, if at all.

Part II

Aligned equity

We propose a new asset class — aligned equity — in which the financial return to the investor is capped by agreement, and the enterprise value generated beyond that cap is directed, by the same agreement, toward defined public goods connected to — or produced by — the company’s operations.

Public benefit here can span four modes, which fall into two families. The first is the enterprise's effect on the world beyond it: creating a net-new public good — the R&D pipeline of a pharmaceutical company, say — or reducing a negative externality it would otherwise impose, such as the carbon intensity of its operations. The second is the distribution of the value the enterprise creates: sharing its upside with the workers and communities who help produce it, or lowering the cost of goods and services to those who depend on them. The instrument is indifferent to which mode a given company pursues; what it fixes is that some defined portion of surplus is directed to one of them rather than to uncapped private return.

The mechanism is deliberately conventional. It requires no new legal entity, no change in corporate law, and no departure from how enterprises are built, financed, or run. An aligned equity investor takes an ordinary equity or equity-like position on standard terms for the stage and sector. To those terms is attached a single addition — a public good annex — that specifies three things: the ceiling on the investor's financial return, the definition of the public good the surplus is intended to fund, and the governance by which that commitment is held over time. Below the cap, the instrument behaves like any other investment. Above it, value that would ordinarily accrue to the investor is redirected to public ends the parties agreed to in advance.

This design resolves the double bind directly. The capital operates inside the enterprise rather than being routed out to a lower-capacity vehicle, so the company's expertise, distribution, and efficiency — the capacities that make it the most effective producer of public goods available — are the very things put to work. The redirection of surplus to public benefit is fixed by contract from the outset, even where the specific benefit can be defined broadly and sharpened as the enterprise matures depending on the stage of investment. And the investor chooses the shape of their return at the outset — how much financial, how much public — without exiting the capital system to do it.

Aligned equity moves the redirection of value upstream, before it ever becomes a matter of discretionary choice at all — embedded contractually at the point where value is created, rather than relying on a downstream collective of individuals to identify a problem and successfully deploy capital against it for public benefit. It depends on no one’s individual temperament because it asks for no change in behavior, only that the contract exists and the agreement be honored. And it avoids the absorption bottleneck because capital flows to enterprise capacity that already exists rather than into a philanthropic system that was never built to hold it.

Aligned equity is novel but not new. Its components — return caps, dual-purpose ownership, contractual redirection of surplus — already exist and have been assembled, bespoke and one enterprise at a time, in cases like Novo Nordisk, Bosch, and Patagonia.10 What has never existed is the infrastructure to make the arrangement standard and accessible by design: templated, replicable, and available to investors in a manner compatible with conventional capital.

Part II · 1

Illustrative examples

Illustrative example #1 — early stage

Aligned equity enters an early-stage round exactly like conventional venture capital: same equity, same risk, and same assumed timeline. The aligned equity instrument adds nothing to the negotiation — the only distinction is the creation of a public good annex, whose agreement comes into effect at the moment of liquidity. The agreed cap is measured against the proceeds of the eventual liquidity event: an acquisition, an IPO, or a later round that lets the position exit. For example, if an enterprise compounds toward an exit 8–10 years out, the conventional capital distributes pro-rata ownership returns indefinitely, while the aligned equity returns stop at the 3.0× cap. Every dollar of value the position would otherwise have captured is redirected to the public good annex.

Illustrative example #2 — growth stage enterprise

At the growth stage, aligned equity sits alongside conventional growth capital, both funding expansion mechanisms. Both positions behave identically until returns are realized — through acquisition, IPO, or refinancing.

This scenario best demonstrates aligned equity's clearest case for scale — the checks are large enough and enterprises durable enough that even a single liquidity event can fund a public good in the hundreds of millions.

Illustrative example #3 — mature enterprise, recapitalization

With a recapitalization, the business is mature and generating cash. The aligned equity investor’s return comes mainly from the ongoing distributions the operations continue to create rather than expectation of significant appreciation like that of an early investment. Therefore, the path to the assigned cap is shorter and the multiple is likely correspondingly lower than early-stage risk. The conventional capital continues to draw distributions from the business indefinitely. The aligned equity capital stops at the cap, with the surplus above that flowing to the public good as specified in the transaction’s agreement.

This is the case that best shows aligned equity doesn't require a funding need or a growth story to be employed — it can invest in an ownership transfer and still commit the business's future cash flow to public purpose.

Part II · 2

Directing surplus

Where an enterprise continues to operate, its public goods are produced through its core operations, as defined above. The question addressed here is narrower: when an investment reaches liquidity and the operating company is absorbed, the surplus value above the investor's cap must be carried to something that outlasts the enterprise itself. The pathways below map how that surplus can be directed.

The forms the redirected surplus might take are illustrated below. They are presented as illustrative rather than prescriptive: the appropriate structure for any given investment depends on the nature of the enterprise, the shape of the liquidity event, and a tax analysis specific to the transaction. What follows maps the design space; it does not specify a mechanism, and every pathway described here would be subject to structuring and tax review before use.

Restricted mission or charitable vehicle

The surplus flows into an earmarked vehicle — a purpose trust, a restricted account, or a charitable entity — established to hold and deploy it toward a defined public benefit.

Public-interest sidecar for intellectual property or data

Rather than moving cash, a nonprofit or public-interest entity holds or licenses the enterprise's intellectual property, model, or dataset, so that an asset a profit-maximizing acquirer would otherwise enclose remains available to the public. Precedents exist across software, data, and patents, from foundation-held applications to open-data trusts and patent pledges. What distinguishes this pathway is that it produces a structural public good rather than a pool of money — it preserves the asset itself.

Covenants that run with the asset

In place of moving value into a separate vehicle, the terms of the transaction bind the acquirer directly: an essential good remains accessible, a price stays below a defined threshold, a model or dataset stays open. The public benefit is embedded in the asset as a restriction that survives the transaction, requiring no external holding entity at all. This is the most direct answer to the durability question — the benefit survives because it is welded to the thing being transferred.

Shared with stakeholders

The surplus is directed to those who helped create the value — an employee liquidity pool, a community trust, or benefit to suppliers or customers.

Advance market commitment or pull mechanism

The surplus funds an advance market commitment, prize, or similar instrument that pulls forward a specific public good — a proven mechanism in domains such as vaccine and essential-medicine markets. It directs capital not at an institution but at an outcome, paying for a defined result once delivered, and can incorporate the speed and execution discipline of the private sector into the production of a public good.

Recycling into an aligned equity capital pool

The surplus does not exit the system. It flows into a pool that redeploys into the next generation of public goods enterprises, so that each liquidity event replenishes the capital available to the next. This pathway turns individual exits into self-propagating infrastructure — capital structured for public benefit funding the creation of more of the same.

Part II · 3

Aligned equity pilot: Kalium Investments

Kalium is an investment platform focused on capital innovation — on better directing resources towards the creation of public goods. Kalium’s purpose is to prove aligned equity at scale by creating — and publicly resourcing — the core infrastructure to support these initial aligned equity investments and liquidity events.

Founded in 2026, Kalium makes the case for aligned equity through three core activities. First, coalescing public-minded asset holders and offering a model for them to more fully express their values beyond financial returns. Second, using that pool of public-minded capital to take conventional LP positions in early stage VC funds in effort to build the operational infrastructure and network trust needed to attract competitive founders. Finally, by making direct investments into founders.

We are going to market with early-stage investments not because aligned equity is suited only to inception investing, but because this is where the most room for experimentation exists and where early trust with GPs and founders meaningfully compounds. That trust carries forward as the same networks build companies at later stages. As we bring together more public minded asset holders, the increased capital pool, established investment infrastructure, and proven founder network will allow us to move up market and demonstrate aligned equity’s potential for public good creation at all stages of company development.

Kalium intentionally holds no proprietary rights to the aligned equity investment structure itself, rather seeks to demonstrate its potential as a method of investing that any public minded asset holder or investment allocator can deploy. Kalium’s success hinges on developing technical expertise — templatizing the legal resources, establishing best governance practices, and navigating seminal liquidity events — and demonstrating the potential of aligned equity through narrative and network development at scale.

Part III

Appendix

Appendix A — Wealth concentration trend

Wealth concentration is not new, but its trajectory over the past three decades is unprecedented. Global billionaire wealth reached $18.3 trillion in 2025, up 16% year-over-year and 81% since 2020. The top 0.001% now hold 6.1% of global wealth, up from 3.7% in 1995 — a share now three times larger than that held by the bottom half of humanity combined.11

Appendix B — The AI liquidity event

The structural trends of wealth concentration explain the scale we’re working with, but the case for building this infrastructure now rests on a particular market urgency: AI driven value creation is compressing what would ordinarily unfold over decades into a small number of discrete, near-simultaneous liquidity events. This is a large opportunity, and we have a narrow window.

Within roughly a twelve-month span: SpaceX completed its IPO at a $1.77 trillion valuation in June 2026 — the largest IPO in history. Anthropic's Series H valued the company at $965 billion in May 2026, up from $183 billion just nine months earlier. OpenAI's confidential IPO filing in June 2026 targets a valuation of $852 billion to over $1 trillion. Combined, these three companies are targeting $3.6–3.8 trillion in enterprise value through their anticipated 2026 IPOs alone.12

That concentration of liquidity events implies a corresponding concentration of potential philanthropic liquidity. One independent analyst estimates $370 billion in philanthropic capital becoming liquid as a direct result of these transactions — including $220 billion tied to the OpenAI Foundation, $90 billion in pledged wealth from Anthropic's founders, and $60 billion held in Anthropic employee DAFs — which, if fully deployed, would represent a $37–100 billion annual increase in new giving, a 6–17% increase over all current US philanthropy.13

Liquidity does not necessitate distribution. As the DAF data earlier in this paper shows, capital that becomes liquid without a structure obligating its redirection tends to sit without incentive to move. Absent a mechanism that captures this surplus contractually, there is no assurance that this wave of AI-driven wealth will behave differently than the DAF assets already accumulating faster than they're granted out.

Appendix C — Structural case studies

The below case studies demonstrate that novel public-benefit mechanisms can work at real commercial scale, but each example remains bespoke because no infrastructure exists to templatize and deploy them beyond the single company that built them. Incentive misalignment keeps these solutions bespoke, as no single company captures enough of the benefit itself to justify building that infrastructure alone. Additionally, capital deployment plans typically get built reactively, at the moment of a liquidity event, rather than being embedded into a company's capital structure from formation.

Closing this gap requires both technical assets (legal templates, governance structures) and field-shaping work (compelling examples, narrative shift). That is the explicit design target of aligned equity: the standardized infrastructure that lets any stage or sector of a company adopt this infrastructure at scale.
